NORWAY · ELECTRIC FERRIES

An electric ferry saves its owner money. It saves the rest of us more.

What one battery ferry is worth in a year, when you also count the pollution it avoids.

Norway runs more than a hundred car ferries. When one of them switches from diesel to batteries, the owner pays more up front but saves on fuel every year. That shows up in the accounts.

What does not show up is the diesel that is never burned: about 3,300 tonnes of CO₂ a year for a typical ferry.

Who gets the money?

Over a ferry's 30-year life, the value lands in three places. Only two of them get paid.

– to shipyards, designers and battery makers, paid once
– to the ferry owner in lower running costs
– to society in avoided climate damage. No one books it.

Across the 84 ferries in the study, the suppliers' share alone is about €384 million.

Why investors should care

Some investors already put a price on impact. Summa Equity, for example, converts the climate effect of its companies into euros, using the same IFVI method as above.

Seen that way, the companies that design, build and electrify ferries are not just suppliers. They sit where much of the value is created.

How this is calculated, and sources
  • Owner's gain = running costs saved − extra investment spread over 30 years at 7.1% (as in the study).
  • Climate gain = (diesel × 3.206 t CO₂ per tonne − CO₂ from the electricity used) × carbon price. Climate only; jobs and health are not counted.
  • Typical ferry = averages for 84 battery ferries in Siemens/Bellona, "Seven out of ten Norwegian ferries…": 89,000 t diesel and 275,000 t CO₂ saved a year, €384m extra investment, €77m lower running costs a year, 237 GWh electricity. The study is older; prices have changed since.
  • €218 ≈ the IFVI/VBA value of $236 per tonne for 2023 (summary). €75 is an approximate recent EU carbon price, not a quote.
  • Summa's approach: impact accounting.
  • Illustrative averages, not any operator's accounts. The 30-year split is undiscounted, for scale only.
